Research Plan: Mesocosm Experiments on Wildfire Ash, Smoke Shading, and Optical Clarity in Lake Tahoe

Author

Dante Capone

Published

March 17, 2026

1 Project Overview

This research investigates how wildfire smoke and ash affect optical clarity in Lake Tahoe through both direct optical effects and indirect ecological pathways mediated by microbial communities. Using a 28-day replicated mesocosm experiment, we will separately manipulate (1) UV-biased smoke shading that selectively reduces UV-B relative to PAR, and (2) ash additions spanning field-realistic deposition to upper-bound hotspot pulses. The core experimental design explicitly separates and recombines these two wildfire-linked drivers to identify dominant mechanisms and test whether microbial community responses mediate clarity changes.

Lake Tahoe clarity is governed by fine particles in the 1–6 µm range, with picoplankton (<2 µm) representing a potentially undermonitored contributor. This project focuses on microbially mediated pathways linking wildfire disturbance to particle formation, aggregation, and optical attenuation. By combining intensive early sampling (hours to days) with longer-term monitoring (weeks), we will resolve rapid optical responses, community reorganization, and recovery dynamics. Results will directly inform Tahoe clarity management by quantifying wildfire impacts and identifying whether direct particle scattering or indirect biological responses dominate clarity loss.

Central Research Question: How do wildfire ash deposition and smoke-induced UV-biased shading affect Lake Tahoe optical clarity through direct optical effects versus indirect microbially mediated pathways?

Experimental Approach: Factorial mesocosm experiment (4 shading × 4 ash levels, n=5–8 replicates, 28 days)

Primary Response Variables: Optical clarity (beam attenuation, Secchi proxy), microbial community composition (16S/18S rRNA), particle size distribution (0.5–20 µm), metabolism (GPP/ER), nutrients

Management Relevance: Quantifies wildfire impacts on Tahoe’s primary management indicator (clarity) and identifies mechanistic pathways for TMDL modeling and forecasting

Timeline: 28-day core experiment with 3 phases: pulse response (days 0–3), community reorganization (days 4–14), recovery (days 15–28)

8.2 Mesocosm Setup

We will deploy cylindrical limnocorral mesocosms in Lake Tahoe using in-lake flexible “bag” enclosures open to the atmosphere. This approach preserves oligotrophic lake temperature, light regime, and microbial inoculum while allowing controlled manipulations and adequate replication.

Code 
flowchart TD
    A[Floating Frame] --> B[UV-Blocking Film +<br/>Shade Cloth]
    A --> C[Limnocorral Bag<br/>1500-2500 L]
    
    B --> D[PAR/UV Radiometer<br/>Surface]
    C --> E[Open to Atmosphere]
    E --> F[Subsurface Sensor<br/>0.5 m]
    C --> G[DO + Temp Logger<br/>1 m]
    C --> H[Disc Plunger Mixer<br/>2-3× Daily]
    C --> I[Sampling Port<br/>0-2 m Integrated]
    C --> J[Sealed Bottom]
    
    style A fill:#95e1d3,color:#000
    style C fill:#4ecdc4,color:#fff
    style G fill:#ffe66d,color:#000

Physical specifications:

  • Volume: 1,500–2,500 L per mesocosm
  • Geometry: ~1 m diameter, 2–3 m depth
  • Material: UV-transparent flexible enclosure (limnocorral bag)
  • Location: In-lake deployment on floating frames in Lake Tahoe
  • Water source: Depth-integrated epilimnetic water (0–10 m) from Lake Tahoe
  • Mixing: Gentle mechanical mixing using disc plunger 2–3× daily to prevent stratification artifacts and unrealistic particle settling
  • Temperature: In-lake deployment provides shared thermal forcing; shading will alter heat inputs as part of smoke mechanism (measured, not eliminated)

8.3 Duration and Phases

Total duration: 28 days (defensible core experiment duration based on Tahoe wildfire response monitoring showing ~2-week recovery lags)

Phases:

  1. Equilibration (Days -2 to 0): 48-hour acclimation after filling mesocosms; baseline temperature, DO, and light profiles recorded
  2. Pulse response (Days 0–3): Ash addition on Day 0; shading onset; intensive sampling at +6h, +24h, +48h, +96h to capture rapid optical and chemical responses
  3. Community reorganization (Days 4–14): Episodic shading events (3–8 day episodes with clear intervals); sampling on days 7, 10, 14 to resolve microbial succession and particle dynamics
  4. Recovery and persistence (Days 15–28): Continued monitoring with reduced shading frequency; sampling on days 21, 28 to test recovery trajectories and legacy effects

Extending to 28 days allows observation of microbial succession and particle aggregation/settling while minimizing mesocosm wall effects.

8.4 Shading Treatment Design

Rationale: Smoke shading should be treated as UV-biased and episodic rather than only neutral-density constant shade.

Treatment levels:

  • Control (no shading): Full ambient light (100% PAR, 100% UV-B)
  • Low shading: ~10–15% PAR reduction, ~30% UV-B reduction (simulates light smoke)
  • Medium shading: ~25–35% PAR reduction, ~50% UV-B reduction (simulates moderate smoke)
  • High shading: ~50% PAR reduction, ~65–80% UV-B reduction (simulates heavy/dense smoke)

Implementation:

  • Shading material: Combination of UV-blocking film (for selective UV-B suppression) and neutral-density shade cloth (for PAR reduction)
  • Episodic regime (primary): 3–8 day shading events with clear intervals, matching observed California smoke event structure
  • Continuous variant (optional): Single treatment with sustained shading to test extreme sustained smoke hypothesis
  • Light verification: Radiometer measurements of incident PAR and UV-B at surface plus standardized subsurface depth (e.g., 0.5 m) to verify treatment targets

Evidence basis: Castle Lake smoke studies reported incident UV-B and PAR reductions of 31% and 11% (light smoke) to 53% and 28% (moderate smoke), with underwater reductions exceeding surface values. Tahoe smoke monitoring indicates ~50% PAR reduction in extreme events, supporting the high-shading tier.

8.5 Ash Treatment Design

Rationale: Ash dosing should include both field-realistic deposition-equivalent treatments and upper-bound mechanistic hotspot pulses.

Treatment levels:

  • Control (no ash): No ash addition (0 mg/L)
  • Deposition-equivalent (low): ~0.1–1 mg/L - field-realistic deposition based on Tahoe 2021 monitoring (~240 mg/m²/day)
  • Moderate hotspot (medium): ~10 mg/L - mechanistic pulse representing near-surface accumulation
  • High hotspot (upper-bound): ~25–50 mg/L - upper-bound mechanistic treatment matching Tahoe/Castle incubation experiments

Dose conversion: Areal load (g/m²) to water-column concentration (mg/L) = (g/m² × 1000) / (depth in m × 1000). Example: 2 g/m² over 2 m depth ≈ 1 mg/L mesocosm-average.

Ash source and preparation:

  • Source: Wildfire ash collected from recent California fires, or laboratory-generated standardized material if field collection is infeasible
  • Pyrolysis fractionation: Separate less-pyrolyzed (“brown”) and more-pyrolyzed (“gray”) fractions where feasible, as Tahoe rapid-response work shows systematic differences in nutrient leachability
  • Particle size: Prioritize fine fractions (<10 µm) as Tahoe clarity is dominated by small particles
  • Leachate preparation:
    • Water-extractable (WE): DI water shake ~16 h, centrifuge, decant (immediate bioavailable nutrients)
    • Easily oxidizable (EO, optional): H₂O₂ oxidation at 70–80°C for 5 days, stop with sodium sulfite (medium-term release)
  • Characterization: Elemental composition (C, N, P, metals), particle size distribution, leachate chemistry (DIN, SRP, TP, DOC)

Application method:

  • Whole ash: Dry deposition to mesocosm surface as areal load (g/m²), then gentle mixing
  • Leachate-only control: Filtered WE leachate added without particles to isolate chemical fertilization from particle optics
  • Inert particle control (optional): Combusted and rinsed mineral surrogate to isolate scattering from nutrient effects

8.6 Controls

Experimental controls:

  • True control: No ash, no shading (ambient conditions)
  • Leachate-only control: WE leachate without particles (isolates chemical fertilization from particle optics)
  • Inert particle control (optional): Combusted mineral surrogate (isolates scattering from nutrient effects)
  • Procedural controls: Handling controls for all sampling and addition procedures
  • Positive control (optional): Small N+P enrichment to validate microbial responsiveness in oligotrophic conditions

Total number of mesocosms:

  • Ash levels: 4 (control, deposition-equivalent, moderate hotspot, high hotspot)
  • Shading levels: 4 (control, low, medium, high)
  • Full factorial: 4 × 4 = 16 treatment combinations
  • Replicates per treatment: 5–8
  • Total: 80–128 mesocosms

Blocking:

  • Spatial blocking: Mesocosms arranged in randomized blocks on floating frames to account for spatial gradients in light, temperature, or water chemistry
  • Randomization: Treatment assignment randomized within blocks to minimize confounding
  • Temporal blocking: All mesocosms filled within same day using same source water; treatments applied simultaneously on Day 0

Rationale: Tahoe wildfire-response incubations used n=5 replicates successfully. Microbial community composition shows high variability, so n=6–8 is preferred for PERMANOVA power. Published low-productivity mesocosm studies used n=5 as conventional minimum.

8.9 Measured Response Variables

Primary response variables (Tahoe-relevant):

  1. Optical clarity
    • Beam attenuation (670 nm): Primary optical metric matching Tahoe wildfire-response monitoring
    • PAR and UV attenuation (Kd): Depth-integrated light profiles
    • Secchi depth proxy: Where feasible in mesocosm geometry
    • Spectral absorption/scattering: Separate CDOM vs particulate contributions
  2. Microbial community (focus on picoplankton <2 µm)
    • Bacterial/archaeal abundance: Flow cytometry targeting undermonitored picoplankton fraction
    • Community composition: 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (≥20,000 reads/sample)
    • Protist community: 18S rRNA sequencing (optional, microeukaryotes)
    • Functional genes: Metatranscriptomics if budget allows (nutrient cycling, DOM production)
  3. Phytoplankton
    • Chlorophyll a: Fluorometric quantification
    • Community composition: Flow cytometry or microscopy
    • Primary production: ¹⁴C uptake or O₂ evolution

Secondary response variables:

  1. Particle dynamics (optically important size classes)
    • Particle size distribution: 0.5–20 µm (LiQuilaz 16 size classes, Tahoe-compatible)
    • Particle concentration: Focus on 1–6 µm range (Tahoe clarity-relevant)
    • POC/PN: Particulate organic matter stoichiometry
    • EPS/TEP: Aggregation indicators (transparent exopolymer particles)
  2. Dissolved organic matter
    • DOC concentration: Total dissolved organic carbon
    • CDOM absorption: Colored DOM spectral properties
    • DOM quality: Fluorescence EEMs if feasible
  3. Nutrients and chemistry
    • DIN: NH₄⁺ + NO₃⁻ (dissolved inorganic nitrogen)
    • SRP: Soluble reactive phosphorus
    • TP/TN: Total phosphorus and nitrogen
    • pH, alkalinity, conductivity: Basic water chemistry
  4. Metabolism (oxygen-based)
    • GPP: Gross primary production from DO time series
    • ER: Ecosystem respiration
    • NEP: Net ecosystem production (GPP - ER)

10.1 Mixed-Effects Models

Approach: Use linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) to test treatment effects on continuous response variables.

Model structure:

Response ~ Ash * Shading + (1|Block) + (1|Time)

Fixed effects:

  • Ash treatment (categorical or continuous dose)
  • Shading treatment (categorical or continuous)
  • Interaction term (Ash × Shading)

Random effects:

  • Block (if applicable)
  • Time (repeated measures)
  • Mesocosm ID (nested within treatment)

Software: [e.g., R packages lme4, nlme, glmmTMB]

Post-hoc tests: [e.g., Tukey HSD, Dunnett’s test for comparison to control]

10.2 Multivariate Microbial Community Analyses

Approach: Use ordination and permutational tests to assess treatment effects on community composition.

Methods:

  • Ordination: NMDS or PCoA based on Bray-Curtis or weighted UniFrac distances
  • Permutational MANOVA: PERMANOVA to test treatment effects
  • Indicator species analysis: Identify taxa associated with specific treatments
  • Diversity metrics: Alpha diversity (richness, Shannon, Simpson), beta diversity

Software: [e.g., R packages vegan, phyloseq, DESeq2 for differential abundance]

10.3 Optical Clarity Response Analyses

Approach: Separate direct optical effects from indirect ecological effects.

Direct effects:

  • Immediate changes in light attenuation due to ash particles
  • Measured within hours to days of ash addition
  • Compare to particle size distribution and concentration

Indirect effects:

  • Changes in light attenuation due to biological responses (phytoplankton, DOM, microbial activity)
  • Measured over days to weeks
  • Linked to microbial and phytoplankton dynamics

Statistical tests:

  • Time-series analysis to identify lag times
  • Path analysis or structural equation modeling (SEM) to link treatments → microbes → clarity

10.4 Interaction Testing

Approach: Test for synergistic or antagonistic interactions between ash and shading.

Methods:

  • Interaction terms in mixed-effects models
  • Factorial ANOVA
  • Visualization of interaction plots
  • Calculate interaction coefficients (e.g., deviation from additive expectation)

10.5 Mediation and Pathway Analyses

Approach: Test whether microbial community changes mediate the effect of treatments on optical clarity.

Methods:

  • Structural equation modeling (SEM)
  • Mediation analysis (e.g., Baron & Kenny approach, bootstrapping)
  • Path diagrams: Treatment → Microbes → DOM/Particles → Clarity

12 Potential Pitfalls and Alternative Strategies

12.1 Pitfall 1: Mesocosm Artifacts

Risk: Mesocosms may not accurately represent lake conditions (e.g., wall effects, lack of grazing, altered mixing).

Mitigation:

  • Use large mesocosms with high surface area to volume ratio
  • Include gentle mixing to prevent stratification
  • Compare mesocosm baseline to lake conditions
  • Acknowledge limitations in interpretation

Alternative strategy: Complement mesocosm experiments with in situ enclosures or whole-lake observations.

12.2 Pitfall 2: Ash Variability

Risk: Ash composition varies by fire type, fuel, and combustion conditions.

Mitigation:

  • Characterize ash thoroughly (elemental composition, leachate chemistry)
  • Use standardized ash or multiple ash sources
  • Test dose-response to capture variability

Alternative strategy: Focus on mechanistic responses to ash components (e.g., nutrients, particles) rather than specific ash types.

12.3 Pitfall 3: Low Replication for Microbial Endpoints

Risk: High variability in microbial community data may reduce statistical power.

Mitigation:

  • Prioritize higher replication (≥5 per treatment)
  • Use robust statistical methods (e.g., permutational tests)
  • Focus on strong, consistent patterns rather than subtle differences

Alternative strategy: Pool samples or use hierarchical models to account for variability.

12.4 Pitfall 4: Short Duration

Risk: 28 days may not capture long-term or legacy effects.

Mitigation:

  • Focus on acute responses and initial trajectories
  • Extend monitoring if resources allow
  • Acknowledge temporal limitations

Alternative strategy: Conduct follow-up experiments or link to long-term monitoring data.

12.5 Pitfall 5: Scaling to Whole-Lake

Risk: Mesocosm results may not scale to whole-lake dynamics.

Mitigation:

  • Use mesocosm results to parameterize models
  • Compare to historical lake responses to disturbances
  • Collaborate with modelers to scale findings

Alternative strategy: Use mesocosm data to generate hypotheses for whole-lake studies.
